# Which divination practices are open to outsiders (X1)

Researched 2026-10-29 for ed12. Sources are linked in each section; "not verified" marks anything taken from a summary rather than a primary text.

## The short answer

None of the four leads in the old parking lot is ready to become a tool. Ifá, the Shona hakata, southern African bone throwing and Malagasy sikidy are held by trained, initiated or spirit-called diviners, and no keeper has published an open key. Tibetan Mo is more open than the repo's earlier note said, but its answer text is a copyrighted Buddhist work, so the app still should not reproduce it. The most open candidate found is the Chinese moon blocks (jiaobei), which lay worshippers throw themselves. The K'iche' Maya day meanings are a model of how keepers share on their own terms: a group of daykeepers published them, with permission, through the Smithsonian.

The app's design already lowers the risk: the person writes every meaning, so a throw or a layout carries no tradition's answers. The risk that remains is in three places: naming a tool after a closed practice, reproducing its corpus (names of signs, verses, answer tables), or describing the app's own tools as if they were that practice.

## The owner's decision (2026-10-29)

The practices marked **Closed (owner, 2026-10-29)** below are closed: the app does not build them, and the app does not mention them anywhere (no tool, no source entry, no origin note, no credit line, no AI prompt). They are named only in these notes, so that a later session knows why they are absent. The other entries are unchanged.

## What "open" means here

- **Open:** the keepers teach it to anyone, or it is a folk practice anyone may do without a priest or teacher.
- **Partly open:** the keepers share some of it publicly (history, calendar, general meaning) and keep the practice itself for the trained.
- **Closed:** it is learned by initiation, apprenticeship or a spirit's calling, and the keepers have not shared a key for outsiders.

### Ifá

Ifá is inscribed by UNESCO as the "Ifa divination system" (Representative List, 2008, file 00146). UNESCO names its keepers as Ifá priests (babaláwo) in Yoruba communities and the diaspora, says the knowledge is "transmitted among Ifa priests", and names the ageing of priests and the difficulty of training successors as threats. The corpus (16 principal odù making 256, with their verses) is oral, and the priest reads it with the opele chain or the ikin palm nuts on the opon Ifá tray. Initiation is formal and paid. Nothing found offers the odù for outsiders to use.

- Do not name any odù, show an opon or opele, or call the app's 16 geomancy figures odù.
- Closed (owner, 2026-10-29): the app does not mention Ifá at all, including in the geomancy note.
- Sources: [UNESCO, Ifa divination system](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ifa-divination-system-00146); [Wikipedia, Ifá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If%C3%A1) (cites Bascom, *Ifa Divination*, 1969, and Abimbola, 1976).

### Obi

Obi is cast with four pieces of kola (in Yorubaland) or coconut (in the Caribbean and Latin America) and is tied to Ifá and the orisha. Books by practitioners exist (for example Ócha'ni Lele, *Obí Agbón: Lukumí Divination with Coconut*), but who may cast and for whom differs from house to house, and the casting speaks to orisha and ancestors. The app's dice and coins tool already lets a person define a four-piece throw with their own meanings, so nothing is lost by leaving obi out.

- Sources: [Wikipedia, Obi divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obi_divination) (thinly sourced); [Eleda, Obí Agbón](https://eleda.org/product/obi-agbon-lukumi-divination-with-coconut). Who may cast was not verified from a primary text.

### Sikidy

Sikidy is Malagasy divination with seeds laid out in a 16-column table (toetry), related to Arabic geomancy. It is taught by long apprenticeship with a master, with a ritual initiation; many Malagasy people know its rules but say they do not know the art, out of respect for senior diviners. Its mathematics has been studied openly (Marcia Ascher; Marc Chemillier), but study of the mathematics is not permission to practise.

- Closed (owner, 2026-10-29): do not build a toetry tool, and do not name sikidy in the app, including in the geomancy note.
- Sources: [Wikipedia, Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy) (cites Ascher 1997 and 2002, Chemillier et al. 2007, Decary 1970); [EHESS sikidy modelling project](https://ehess.modelisationsavoirs.fr/sikidy/home.html).

### Shona hakata

Hakata are four carved tablets (called "bones") used by the n'anga, Shona healer-diviners, now registered under the Zimbabwe National Traditional Healers Association (ZINATHA). Gelfand (*Shona Religion*, 1962, p. 163) adds a nuance: some lay people also divine with hakata for everyday matters. But the readings are tied to ancestral spirits, and no open key from a keeper was found. The repo's earlier caution stands.

- If the owner ever wants this, the respectful route is to ask ZINATHA or a practising n'anga, not to adapt a book.
- Sources: [Postcolonial Web, The Shona Significance of Bones](https://postcolonialweb.org/zimbabwe/hove/hander2.html) (summarising Gelfand); [Wikipedia, Nganga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nganga); [Manchester Museum of Medicine and Health, divination tablets](https://www.digitalcollections.manchester.ac.uk/view/MH-02008-00112).

### Southern African bone throwing

Sangoma and inyanga throw bones, shells and other objects as healers and spirit mediums; the role comes by an ancestral calling and training. It is closed. The app's charm casting is the app's own design (`lib/charms-core.ts` says so, and no screen calls it bone throwing); keep it that way, and keep words such as "throwing the bones" and "sangoma" out of its copy.

- Source: [Phansi Museum, Throwing the Bones (Google Arts and Culture)](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/jwXR4TVSwdHqeA).

### Tibetan Mo

**A correction to the repo.** The old parking-lot note says Mipham's Mo manual "says it needs initiation from a teacher". The publisher's page, two excerpts the publisher has posted, and Wikipedia's account (citing Goldberg, 1990) do not say that: they describe two throws of a die giving one of 36 answers, "any type of dice" may be used, and the answers are understood to come from Manjushri, whose blessing and mantra the reader relies on. The interior of the book was not read, so the claim is unconfirmed either way. Tibetans broadly consult Mo, and lamas also cast it for others as a service.

- It is still not the app's to reproduce: it is a Buddhist devotional practice, and the 36 answers are a copyrighted translation (Snow Lion, now Shambhala). The current caution in `lib/sources.ts` ("ask a teacher if you want to use it") is right; only the parking-lot note needs correcting.
- Sources: [Shambhala, Mo](https://www.shambhala.com/mo-2630.html); [Snow Lion article, part 1](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/mo-tibetan-divination-system/) and [part 2](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/mo-tibetan-divination-system-2/); [Wikipedia, Mo (divination)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mo_(divination)); [Tibetan Nyingma centre offering Manjushri Mo as a service](https://tngcentre.org/de/our-community/the-office/ceremonies/divination-service-service).

### Jiaobei moon blocks and kau cim

Jiaobei are two crescent blocks, each with a flat and a round side, thrown by worshippers themselves at temples and home shrines in Chinese folk religion, most of all in Taiwan and southern China. There are four landings: one up and one down (shèngjiǎo, yes), both flat (nùjiǎo, no), both round (xiàojiǎo, the gods laugh: unclear or obvious), and a block standing on its edge (lìjiǎo, ask again). A common rule is three yeses in a row. Kau cim (fortune sticks) are shaken from a cup and lead to a numbered verse; each temple has its own verse set.

- This is the most open practice found: no priest is needed. As an app tool it would be a two-piece throw whose four landings the person names and gives meanings to, with the traditional names shown as history, not as answers. It would be a small addition to dice and coins.
- Credit: "moon blocks from Chinese folk religion, thrown by worshippers at temples and home shrines". Keep the question addressed to the person's own practice, not to a named deity.
- Do not reproduce any temple's kau cim verses.
- Sources: [Wikipedia, Jiaobei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiaobei) (thinly sourced; a scholarly source should be found before building, as T17b did for knucklebones); [Wikipedia, Kau cim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kau_cim).

### K'iche' Maya calendar

The ajq'ij (daykeepers) of highland Guatemala are initiated, traditionally on the day 8 B'atz', and divine with tz'ite seeds. The seed divination is theirs. But the meanings of the 20 days were published by the Group of K'iche' Day Keepers Komon Tohil of Zunil, "reproduced with permission", on the Smithsonian's Living Maya Time site. That is what credit and consent look like when keepers choose to share.

- The app could show the day meanings only with Komon Tohil's own permission (the Smithsonian's permission does not pass to the app), and should never build seed divination. A link to the Smithsonian page is fine without permission.
- Sources: [Smithsonian NMAI, The Meaning of the Days in the Maya Sacred Calendar](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/sites/default/files/resources/The%20Meaning%20of%20the%20Days%20in%20the%20Maya%20Sacred%20Calendar.pdf); [Living Maya Time](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar); [Archaeology magazine, The Modern Calendar Priests](https://archaeology.org/issues/november-december-2012/collection/calendar-priests/).

### Kumalak

Kumalak is a Kazakh and Kyrgyz divination with 41 beans or stones laid in a nine-square grid, practised by baqsy (shamans) whom "a spiritual teacher trains and mentors for years". Western kits are sold, but no statement from a keeper about outsiders was found. Leave it out until one is.

- Sources: [Wikipedia, Kumalak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumalak) (thinly sourced); [Signs and symbols of Kazakh culture](https://cultural.kz/?p=3783) (not read).

### Practices already in the app

- **Geomancy.** A written, learned tradition (ʿilm al-raml) that passed into Latin in the 12th century and is taught openly today (Greer, Skinner). Some Muslim scholars disapprove (Ibn Khaldun called it obsolete after the Qur'an), which is worth a line, not a warning. The note in `lib/tool-origins.ts` already says "the related figures of West Africa and Madagascar have their own histories"; leave it as it is. It must not name sikidy or Ifá (owner, 2026-10-29). Source: [Wikipedia, Arabic geomancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_geomancy).
- **Runes and ogham.** Open. One context point: the ADL lists the othala rune (and others) as used by white supremacists while saying runes have legitimate pagan use and must be judged in context. The app uses the plain Elder Futhark forms; keep it from using the winged othala or SS-style forms in any art or picture prompt. Source: [ADL, Othala rune](https://www.adl.org/resources/hate-symbol/othala-rune).
- **Tasseomancy.** The origin note credits British parlour handbooks, which is accurate. Keep the word "gypsy" out of any copy and source labels; the "gypsy fortune-teller" is a stereotype of the Roma, not a credit. No use of it was found in the repo.

## Not divination, but relevant to the oracle kit

The 1993 "Declaration of War Against Exploiters of Lakota Spirituality" (Lakota Summit V, 10 June 1993) says Lakota ceremonies are only for the Lakota, Dakota and Nakota Nations, and Arvol Looking Horse's 2003 proclamation set standards for who may run ceremonies. This is the clearest keeper statement found, and it applies to animal-guide and "medicine" decks in the style of *Medicine Cards*. A search of `lib/`, `app/`, `components/` and `data/` found no "spirit animal", "totem", "medicine wheel" or "vision quest"; keep it that way in the oracle kit, the picture maker's prompts and the companion's wording. Source: [Wikipedia, Inipi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inipi) (cites the declaration and the 2003 proclamation; the full text of the declaration was not found online in this session).

## How to credit, when the app names an open tradition

Closed practices are not credited in the app, because the app does not mention them.

1. Name the people and place, and the keepers by their own word (babaláwo, n'anga, ajq'ij), not "shaman" or "witch doctor".
2. Link the keepers' own statement or institution where there is one (UNESCO, ZINATHA, Komon Tohil through the Smithsonian).
3. Say what the app does not do: "This app does not teach Ifá. Ifá is learned from its priests."
4. Never "ancient secret", "tribal", "exotic", or a claim that the app holds a tradition's meanings.
5. When the app's tool only resembles a practice (charm casting and bone throwing; the two-piece throw and moon blocks), say it is the app's own design.
